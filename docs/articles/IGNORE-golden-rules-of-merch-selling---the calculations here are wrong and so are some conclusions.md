# Golden Rules of Merch Selling/Webshops

This is just a document in which I want to keep track of lessons learned and general guidelines I discovered along the way.

## Lesson 1

**Lesson #1: You're not going to outcompete on price.** To be successful you can either be _the best_ or _the only one_ to do something. A small webshop with print-on-demand is never going to be cheaper, faster, higher quality than the big guys. 

So, 

* Focus on being _unique_. Be recognizable, authentic, the only place where people can find something specific.
* Keep your prices _high_; don't enter the race to the bottom. See Lesson #2.
* Do **not** compromise on authenticity, humanity, creativity, control over your work. 
  * Basically everything _you_ can do that a big large company _can't_. 
  * Even if that means keeping prices quite high, or a very small catalog of products.
  * Because the alternative, as stated, means really low prices or copycat products that give you no profit ... while still being higher-priced than others and uninteresting.

## Lesson 2

**Lesson #2: Be bold.** Have bravado. Be overly confident. Present your shit as if it's the best in the world and then go away without pleading for people to buy.

In practice, this means,

* Succinct titles, descriptions, images, details. They include what the product is and what's special about it, no more.
* Bold colors. Eye-catching visuals.Heavy contrast---perhaps too heavy---if needed. (Pastel, cute soft colors, they're all nice ... but only if you have an established fan base looking for _that_. Otherwise, people will never even notice your stuff.)
* Text is readable even at small thumbnail size. (Most never click to enlarge, and unreadable text on a small mug is never going to interest anyone.)

Assume everyone has seen it all before. They barely give your design any attention, as it flashes by in a tiny size on their screen. What would make them stop? What would make them immediately recognize your "brand"?

## Lesson 3

**Lesson #3: Keep MINIMUM margins of ~7 euros or 25--30%, but never make shipping fees higher than price.**. This is about the average amount from my research. It included reading looooads of forums with people giving their experiences and their struggles, reading all the guides and articles from the POD platforms, and more.

Why, though?

* You need to pay for POD orders in advance, so you want to get a nice "reserve" for that. (Just like usual businesses, of course, where you invest first and need the funds/risk management for that.)
* Over time, prices can go up. Inflation, shipping issues, holiday season surcharge, etcetera. You need more than enough wiggle room to not immediately sell at a loss.
* It allows generous discounts and special events to run. (In a way, you want to be able to discount ANY item by 30% and still never make a loss on it.)
* Because of lesson #2.

For example, I just checked the print cost of Kiss-Cut Stickers on Printful.

* It's €1.95 base cost (excl VAT) 
* (I'll handle shipping later. Let's just assume we put shipping onto the customer for now.)
* How to price these?
  * Adding 7 euros = €8.95, which is ridiculous of course.
  * Adding 30% profit = 1.95*1.3 = €2.50, which is fine, but feels weird when so much lower than shipping.
  * Higher than shipping = €3.50, which I'd prefer the most.

This might seem ridiculous. "3.50 for a single sticker?" 

But what if anything changes? What if, next year, the cost is suddenly raised to €2.95? If you were selling these at €2.50, you're now losing money (45 cents) on every sale. It's unlikely you can be _certain_ you are notified of this change and (can) act accordingly in time to raise prices.

Additionally, sales can be exceedingly _rare_ (especially when starting out), while any webshop still needs to pay recurring costs. Making only 55 cents on a sale vs making 155 cents matters a lot for financial safety in the long run. Now extrapolate that to _all_ products having this nice extra buffer. On 50 orders, for example, you now have 50 euros _more_, which covers any reasonable webshop subscription or server hosting expenses.

In cases like these, though, I want to add a _second_ "rule" for pricing. One that's more complicated and depends on shipping, which is why I made it its own lesson.

I'd rather look at it the other way around: it's not the sticker cost that's weird here, it's that _shipping_ cost. The base cost of every product is usually very reasonable on these platforms, it's the _other things_ that make it bad. To be continued at lesson #4. 

## Lesson 4

**Lesson #4: Sort your main price cycle: printing > shipping > tax.** It has become clear to me that there is no such thing as worldwide shipping or worldwide availability. At least not when you're a (small) webshop using POD. Either shipping (+tax/customs) creates a ludicrous price for the products OR it's printed at the "nearest printer" which means products vary in actual look and quality---which you cannot control.

You have only two options:

* [Recommended for beginners] Purposely go local. Pick a small geographical range and target that specifically. It means low and controllable fees, while those inside the target area are likely to find and prefer your store first.
* [Recommended once you get the hang of it] Purposely split your webshop into multiple "parts" depending on continent. Some products only available in EU. Some only USA. That kind of thing.

The wider you cast your net ... the less likely you are going to stand out and actually be memorable. The wider you cast your net ... the more likely someone all the way in Antarctica wants to order stuff at a ludicrous shipping fee that you can't pay back :p The wider you cast your net ... the more likely you need higher prices, more complicated setups/rules/shipping, and there is going to be an issue _somewhere_ at all times.

The moment I realized I could restrict my shipping zones to just the Netherlands (and neighboring countries) was the moment my calculations and pricing actually started making sense.

As for taxes, make sure you're a registered company and all your tax files are sent in and validated before selling anything. It saves you literally ~20% on _every_ purchase. You are still required to pay tax over everything, though, so you must still relay this cost to the customer.

In the Netherlands specifically, as a "sole proprietorship" (zzp'er), you have a BTW-ID ( = TIN, VAT, TAX ID) and OB-ID ( = Sales Tax) to keep at hand and fill in _everywhere_. Also, many platforms do NOT treat this as a business, but they DO require you to put in the business name instead of your own name. Very confusing.

### The best shipping system?

Don't surprise people at checkout with (high) shipping fees. Research is clear on how almost everyone just leaves then. 

Instead, below is the approach I ended up using (and seems most fair to me):

* Set clear shipping zones in your system---don't ship anywhere else.
* Mark up every product with a portion of its shipping fee.
  * Very cheap products, expected to sell in larger quantities, should mark up ~1/3 of the shipping fee ( = "small percentage")
  * Other products, expected to sell once, should mark up ~2/3 of its shipping fee ( = "large percentage"), and then add their profit margin ON TOP.
* Below a certain threshold price, use a _flat high shipping fee_ that covers almost any shipping fee possible.
* Above the threshold price, use _free shipping_.
* Treat _extremely large orders_ as a special case. (Either ask to contact directly or provide a high shipping fee based on the amount.)

Once you have more data ( = you've regularly received orders), you can estimate exactly how much to mark up to make sure all costs are covered (as long as business doesn't get much worse).

### Example: cheap products

Let's return to our example with the Kiss-Cut Stickers. If we change our shipping model to the new one, we get ...

* It's €1.95 base cost (excl VAT), shipping is €3.49 (every next item €0.05 extra)
* So we'd start thinking about a price of _at least_ 1.95 + 0.33 * 3.49 = €3.10.
* Now let's see our shipping policy.
  * Below say ~30 euros, shipping costs ~7 euro (about average cost to cover a country and its neighbors).
  * Above that, shipping is free.

Now let's see what happens on purchases.
* 1 sticker: COST = 5.44, (CUSTOMER) PAYS = 3.10 + 7 = 10.10
* 2 stickers: COST = 7.44, PAYS = 3.10*2 + 7 = 13.20
* 3 stickers: COST = 9.44, PAYS = 3.10*3 + 7 = 16.30
* 4 stickers: COST = 11.44, PAYS = 3.10*4 + 7 = 19.40
* ...
* 10 stickers: COST = 23.44, PAYS = 3.10*10 (no shipping fee!) = 30.30
* 20 stickers: COST = 43.44, PAYS = 3.10*20 = 62.00

Looking at this,

* For tiny orders, you are comfortably earning a (10.10 - 5.44)/10.10 = ~45% profit margin. 
* For larger orders, you earn a (30.30-23.44)/30.30 = ~25%-30% profit margin.

While giving the simplest and most reasonable shipping structure, you're still earning more than our ideal "minimum". People are more likely to want _multiple_ stickers, and thanks to this structure they are also more likely to actually _buy that_. And you can make them a nice, round €3 per sticker. Not too low, also not high enough to seem ludicrous.

In other words, **start thinking about profit margins on overall (likely) orders, not individual products**.

Despite only trying to cover a _part_ of shipping fees, we still make a profit at all times because shipping fees are HIGHER than product cost!

### Example: expensive products

To complete the example, let's check a more expensive product.

I pulled up a random product on Printful.

* A premium hoodie for base cost €21.
* Shipping is €6.29 (Netherlands), and every next item after the first is €2.00
* Okay, so what price are we thinking about? 
  * A = 21 + 0.67*6.29 (shipping fee mark up)
  * Price = A * 1.3 (profit margin) = €25.21 * 1.3 = €32.77 
  * (Which is ... about 7 euros over cost price! Nice!)

Let's do our fun list of cost and payment again. It's easier this time ;)

* 1 hoodie: COST = 21 + 6.29 = 27.29, (CUSTOMER) PAYS = 32.77 (already no shipping fee!)
* 2 hoodies: COST = 21*2 + 6.29 + 2.00 = 50.29, PAYS = 32.77 * 2 (already no shipping fee!) = 65.54
* 3 hoodies: COST = 21*3 + 6.29 + 2*2.00 = 73.29, PAYS = 32.77 * 3 = 98.31

As you see, our biggest "danger zone" is around that threshold where the shipping fee disappears. But we still keep a profit there. A healthy one of ~25%. Which grows only larger on bigger orders. 

In my experience, an ideal threshold for free shipping (which I've seen more people use) is something like 50 euros. Most merchandise is at a price point where silling 1--3 units will then just creep _under_ that bar.

Most importantly, **how many people are going to buy many hoodies (at once)?** The most likely sale is a single hoodie _or_ a large batch of them in special cases (such as someone giving one to every member of staff/family/an event). In the most likely cases, you have a healthy profit margin---and the edge cases can be a bit lower just fine.

_What if someone buys one of many DIFFERENT items?_ Yes, that's why you still mark up _every single product_ considerably. Because now you'll lose that shipping fee, the first and highest one, on EVERY product. And such an order is likely to exceed our threshold and get free shipping, so we can't recoup anything there.

That's why we get to the final "obvious" lesson: **your base price for a product should NEVER be lower than cost + largest possible shipping fee** (in this structure). Because then you'd lose a LOT of money on orders with only a single one of every item. In our case, 32.77 > 21 + 6.29 = 27.29.

I put "obvious" in quotes because it's _so easy_ to fall for "must price lower, must not scare clients away, must be cheaper". And at first, you might feel like it works. For example, selling that hoodie for €25 means you get a profit on all sales _except_ 2 hoodies (that's just break-even). But if someone orders 1 hoodie ... and 1 of something else ... and 1 of something else ... you are now _paying_ 15 euros to sell that.

Additionally, after aaaaall of this ... you still need to add tax. Also a _not insignificant amount_, especially on larger orders. It's standard to put this separately on the invoice, but still make the customer pay for it. (Because if they're purchasing for business, they can then deduct that tax themselves, bla bla. Or, in other words, it's not actually the _cost_ of the product but the _tax owed to the government_ for this transaction taking place. So it shouldn't be part of the price.)

I wish I could ask lower prices and have no surprises. But I can't, that's just the reality of it, and you can do calculations like this to remind yourself of that fact.

### Conclusion

Anyway, these were the numbers that ended up working for me. Pfew, this section became waaay longer than I intended. I just hope the quick calculations showed how they're somewhere in the right ballpark. To recap,

* Stuff where shipping exceeds cost? ( = cheap small things) Mark up ~1/3 of shipping, ensure this makes price higher than shipping, then check if this still gives profit on most likely case ( = multiple purchases at once)
* More expensive stuff likely to be bought once? Mark up ~2/3 of shipping, then add profit margin of 25%--30% (at least), then check if this gives at least ~7 euros margin on the most likely case ( = 1 purchase)
* Keep a shipping policy similar to "highest possible shipping fee below 50 euros, free shipping above". It works with all kinds of shipping policies that platforms come up with (per item, per item + cheaper for next items, flat rate, flat rate depending on total cost, ...)
* _Extremely_ expensive stuff is a special case anyway, handle that however you like (extreme mark up, contact us, shipping fee returns above certain threshold, etc)

## Lesson 5

**Lesson #5: Keep as much control as possible on your side.** Systems that do stuff for you automatically may seem nice. In practice, though, they often only do _some_ things automatically---and even those can go wrong. Objectively, I've lost more time correcting automatic systems or fighting some surprises put on me, than just inputting products manually for certain systems and thus keeping all control.

For example,

* Some platforms formatted the description wrong when transferring to WordPress. (Example: lists weren't lists anymore.) So I had to painstakingly edit that anyway every darn time.
* Some platforms automatically include a dozen mockup images. This quickly fills your storage limit (or just blows up your cheap server), and I had to often manually remove almost all those mockups I didn't need.
* Most platforms don't allow setting categories, tags, comments, or other metadata you probably want on each product anyway. So, again, lots of manual work for you in the end.

Conclusion? You're not saving time by letting things happen automatically and/or locking yourself into some platform's way of doing things. Keep control in your own hands wherever you can.

## Lesson 6

**Lesson #6: Trace everything back to one single point of access.** Make sure you only need _one_ bank account/credit card/whatever to pay and receive all finances for the entire webshop. 

Why?

* Less work to set up, maintain and access for you.
* Less chance of making errors, forgetting to refill a balance, etcetera.
* Because profit comes in on the same account as payment, it will likely never run out and leave orders pending.

Similarly, you ideally have a single system for managing the rest. That is, a single "webshop manager" (e.g. WooCommerce, Snipcart) and a single "payment provider" (e.g. Mollie, Stripe, Paypal). Adding multiple things always feels enticing, as you feel like you're covering more ground, giving customers more options, and so forth. In reality, it's major headaches for you without leading to any tangible benefit.

Pick one system, one financial provider, one approach, and stick to it.

## Lesson 7

**Lesson #7: (Almost) all guides, tips-and-tricks, courses, and paid tools are bullshit.** Most articles I encountered when researching tips for how to do this ... just listed the same vague, non-committal, or flat-out _wrong_ clichés. I assume most were written by an AI, or a severely underpaid intern copy-pasting. 

No, you do NOT want to stuff your webshop full of keywords. Or "power words" that are "emotional", or whatever. This makes the experience a nightmare for customers, while search engines have long ago learned to ignore that. It's not authentic. Humans see through it too and assume a bot made this product.

Remember lesson #1: your biggest asset as a POD/small webshop is _being unique and authentic and NOT an army of bots_. Use that.

Similarly, _as usual_, POD or webshops are not a get-rich-quick scheme. You might not have a single sale for the first 6 months. In fact, it's likely. Whenever someone tries to sell you the idea that you'll be swimming in money soon when doing _anything_, they're just padding their own pockets with nonsense.

Yes, marketing is important. Yes, social media helps. Well-written copy and high-resolution images are nice too. But none of the things I found were ever _specific_ enough---like how I try to make articles like these specific with examples and numbers---to actually apply. 

_How_ do you market _your specific products_ successfully on _your social media of choice_? You'll never find the answer to that specific situation. You have to discover it yourself.

As usual, most lessons are learned by simply _doing_. Try things, make mistakes, get better.

I basically erected the first version of my first webshop in a few days. A few frantic days, working 10 hours a day, trying lots of stuff, redoing products multiple times, checking on other platforms, rewriting my shipping policy _again_, etcetera. But within a week, I had a solid understanding of most principles needed, and I would like to say my shop (and webshop management) was professional and clean.

A week. Can you spare a week of experimenting and failing to learn all this and waaaay more by yourself? To learn it in a way you will never forget again? I think anyone can.

## Lesson 8

**Lesson #8: Go forth and create.** And so, don't do this expecting money, because most (creative) pursuits do not lead there. If, instead, you create a webshop by playing around and having fun with designs and possibilities, you won't be disappointed if it takes a while to make sales. You can keep going, keep learning, keep getting better, and the only "cost" is ... having fun designing the next T-shirt?
