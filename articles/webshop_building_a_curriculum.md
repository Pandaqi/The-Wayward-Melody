# Webshop: Building a Curriculum

Some time ago I launched my first [large online store](https://thewaywardmelody.com). The idea started as a way to generate an actual income from the things I make (such as books, games, music), as I'd been mostly giving it away for free until then. 

I struggled, however, to figure out _what_ to sell. To figure out the general theme and vision, which would help me choose from the millions of options of products to make. 

Should I create seasonal products, such as escape rooms themed around Easter, Christmas, etcetera? Should I create fantasy products, such as stories and puzzles around magic, wizards and more? I can easily write down 100 ideas for escape rooms, but I need to _choose_---so what do I choose?

Over time, I kept returning to the one thing that has bothered me since I was a little kid: our systems of education. I mean this literally. One of my earliest memories is my absolute hatred for school and how I started to research how we could improve it.

As such, over the years, I'd already accidentally done a lot of "prep work". I had written [countless (free) tutorials](https://pandaqi.com/tutorials/) of high quality. I had researched how our brains learn. I had written down 50 solid ideas for a line of "educational games" (both video games and board games).

It felt like I had to pull the trigger and actually _make this_. Actually _show_ how much better and more fun learning could be, at every age or grade level, and help people achieve that.

And so I repurposed the webshop to put educational content front and center. I researched curriculae around the world even more heavily, and started to chisel a concrete plan out of the _mountain_ of things to do and to teach.

This "diary" talks about that process. I write down my intentions, the work I did, problems I encountered and how I solved them. As always, this is both a kind of "history" for the project for archiving, and a way to help me struggle through tough problems ;)

## The General Idea

I explained this several times in [other articles](@TODO: link to the "funnel" article), but here's the summary again.

Almost every topic is best explained and deeply learned through **games**. I am absolutely certain of this after all those years.

The problem is that most people are _not_ certain of that. In fact, they call me insane, as they shove "traditional" educational methods down children's throats.

The original plan was to create an entire curriculum of just games. But I now saw that most people would scoff at that and not give it a chance. I needed to gently _nudge_ people away from the old methods (mostly ineffective or even harmful) and towards other ways to teach.

And so I set up the webshop as a kind of "funnel".

* At the start, you have your traditional **Teaching Resources**. I do my best to align these with curriculae and general educational policies around the world, and they are mostly intended for use in a traditional classroom.
* But ... that same topic usually also gets one or more **Quizzes**. They are a slightly more fun way to engage with the material ( + test comprehension), while most people would not object to giving one a try.
* But ... that same topic also has **Puzzles**. They are, again, a better way to engage with that material ( + test comprehension). Once people have seen the power of fun interactie quizzes, they might give one of these a try.
* But ... that same topic also has **"Escape Rooms"**. They are, again, an even better and more creative/rich way to engage with that material. Once people see the fun and effectiveness in one puzzle, it's not a big leap to try a _collection of creative puzzles_ ( = an Escape Room)
* But ... now we've basically arrived at full-fledged **Games**! Once someone has experienced an escape room with their class, for example, it's a really small step to play (board) games around a topic. And then we've finally brought everyone to where they should be!

This is _a lot of work_. It takes creativity, effort, and all my experience to turn any topic into the steps of this funnel. But it _can be done_. As I write this, I've already made quite some materials, and it has _always been possible_. (I mostly write this start of the diary now to take a break from creating all these things 24/7.)

This is also _something unique_. No other educational store/resource does anything close to this. It's uniquely suited to my own skill-set and personality, while offering something very different from major "teacher resource websites" (such as Twinkl).

Finally, this allows the store to _also be useful outside of education_. Sure, the Teaching Resources will only be used in classrooms or by homeschooling parents. But the puzzles, the escape rooms, the games, the stories, they're all widely available and can be enjoyed by anyone for any reason. And because I start with the Teaching Resource first, I usually have a good foundation from which the other variations can "spawn".

## Remembering what makes us different

This is the thing I kept telling myself, whenever I was in doubt or had to make a decision. _Remember what makes your approach to education / this online store UNIQUE._

Over time, I realized the following list of unique points. It started as observations of what I was already doing, then turned into a sort of "tenets" to follow.

* **Diverse**. My interests have always been extremely broad. For example, most kids are clearly "more language-interested" or "more number-interested". I have always been both in equal measure. (I am both a mathematical engineer and an author in many genres!) As such, I include many possible perspectives and approaches with every topic. 
  * I should prefer a varied array of challenges over just repeating the same approach (even if it works well!)
  * That's why most of my Teaching Resources introduce fun exercises, but only do them for 1--3 pages. Because then I want to switch to a different perspective on the topic again.
* **Minimalist**. I have always been poor, and working with _very_ limited means. I'm going to be extremely minimalist when it comes to ink, and paper, and requirements. I'm not spamming the store with 100s of resources around the same general thing, but just create 1 or 2 streamlined packets to do it all. Similarly, everything is as _textless_ as possible, using images and intuitive design to make the exercise work. 
  * There are many wonderful teaching workbooks online that look really professional ... and also include mountains of tiny text on every page to tell the teacher/parent exactly what to do and some more. I know that _I_ would go insane (both as the kid learning or the parent doing this) from that, and so I've always put an effort into shortening texts or removing them altogether if possible.
  * This also makes material independent of language (or color, or paper size) most of the time.
  * Finally, this makes products smaller and more modular. It prevents selling a 100 euro bundle with EVERYTING inside, and instead allows you to buy just the one individual thing you need for 3 euros.
* **Fantastical**. I try to add that fantasy, magical, imaginative, wonderful flair to everything. I'd rather add funny jokes and silly magic ideas, than provide another really abstract or generic resource for kids. Even if this makes things slightly more "messy" or "obscured"---it's a balance I feel I can achieve.
* **The Why**. I always explain _why_ we're even doing this. I try to set up resources to naturally _lead_ learners to the Why, by actually applying the thing in practice. (See why I like puzzles and games as actual methods of learning? To play a game around language, say Scrabble, you need to actively _apply_ language in practice. You both learn, and practice, and get confirmation that the thing you're learning _can be used for stuff_.)
* **Sharing**. I'm not in this to make money. If I could survive without income, I would provide all these resources for free. Many other educational projects, unfortunately, do not share this sentiment. Let's set the right example by lowering prices as much as we can, by being generous with sharing or (re)using these, but also by linking to other valuable resources of "competitors" whenever sensible. It's about sharing. It's about helping people have fun and learn, and it saddens me that I need to ask for some money in return.

Sometimes I had to kill ideas that other "educational resources" use almost 100% of the time, just because they went against these tenets. 

But that's okay, because why would I repeat things that others are already doing (and possibly doing _better_)? More importantly, why would I do it when I have ample evidence it's not actually a great thing to do?

{{% example %}}
Many online resources I found had almost exclusively the same exercise for learning language: you get a big page with an alphabet letter, now color it in/trace it. Repeat 26 times, for all letters.

This is a fine exercise ... once or twice. But the lack of variety means it's both boring and merely trains kids to do that one exercise. A wide variety of challenges is needed to make knowledge deep and broadly applicable; if you repeat identical challenges, even if they're good ones, you merely get practiced at doing that specific challenge.
{{% /example %}}

### Mathematics

Really, mathematics at this stage mostly means "logic" instead. At time of writing, I'm still not sure if I should actually split the two subjects on the website. (As "logic" can surely have important topics at later levels, such as digital skills/programming.)

This subject was quite smooth sailing, and I expect it to mostly stay that way. I have a degree in Applied Mathematics, and I've been programming since I was 11, so I have no shortage of number problems and logic problems to throw at children ;)

#### Recognition & Discrimination

The very first material I made was **Recognition & Discrimination**. I'd learned, from creating my curriculum of games, that this was absolutely the first skill one should engage with _and_ that it's needed to make all puzzles/games possible at all.

This went smoothly, for the most part. 

* At the time, I had just bought a new PC and was waiting on delivery. (The old just couldn't do anything anymore after 12 years of use.) All I could manage to do was _write stories_, so that's why this one has 4 fairy tales, and they're the very first thing I made.
* My only issue with the _teaching resources_ was the original order I intended. I had placed the exercises of "group animals on their preferred terrain" and "place furniture in the right rooms" quite early, and right after one another. Now that I saw the other exercises before me, I realized these were too hard and should be simplified and placed at the end.
* When creating the _quizzes_, I had a debate with myself about whether to make them digital or physical. I chose physical, mostly to keep young kids away from screens and because my digital system wasn't entirely ready just yet.
* Creating the _puzzles_ was a lot of work, but didn't give me any surprises. I merely cut down the Zoo Shuffle to 20 (from 25), purely because I felt the final ones were too complicated and I try to be minimal in any case. (That's the benefit of having made puzzles/games for a long time, and being able to program algorithms to generate 100% solvable and checked puzzles for you.)
* The _escape room_ had to be _so simple_, that it's just 4 pages and was barely any work to create. Still, I think it's quite a good one and young children will understand and like it.

Remember that this skill is just "can you recognize if A and B are the same or different?" (And, at later puzzles/more advanced exercises, "can you connect the same elements/disconnect different elements?")

It's very basic. It means I had to figure out things that required no text or numbers, no complex explanation, no skills a child would not have yet (such as writing). But it was a great exercise in circumventing those restrictions, which strengthened my ideas that came after.

#### My First Shapes & Colors

I separated this from "discrimination" to keep both resources streamlined and standalone. 

Because yes, the Discrimination one also uses a few shapes and colors. But they're only the simplest ones and they're always identical. And, in any case, it's actually good to have topics be interconnected as it helps with learning and association. I just had to be mindful not to break my own rules and use too many (different) shapes and colors.

In _this_ resource, though, you actually see more of them and start to name them. Small differences between them become important. More finetuned puzzles are possible, because we _only_ focus on these subcategories. (For example, a Sudoku-like puzzle about filling in SQUARES and CIRCLES, or one matching colors.)

### Language

Language ... oh boy how I struggled with this one. Maybe it's because I taught myself to read and write, so I couldn't actually tap into my own experiences learning this. Maybe it's because I'm a published author and wanted to _really do it justice_. But the plans changed, and changed again, and I ended up with a huge list of sources and studies, and eventually I had to make some tough choices on how to present it all.

The _initial_ plan was to teach **All Letters (including Phonics & Alphabet Order) > First Words** in Level 1 language, and that's all.

When I made this plan concrete, and did more research, I realized I needed to split "All Letters" into multiple parts. It became **Phonics > Letters > Alphabet Order > First Words**.

Happy with this general plan---and having enough ideas on how to turn that into stories/puzzles/whatever---I started work. 

Until ... I realized I needed to become much more granular again, and take steps more slowly. It's far too quick to go from "no idea about language at all" to "recognize letters and their sounds" in a _single_ resource/product. Especially when resources are usually 10--20 pages long, this one would be 50 pages and just a downright mess! Splitting that one thing in _two_ doesn't help much either.

And so, after more exhaustive rewriting and research, I settled on the following _for Level 1_.

* Sound Recognition (simply recognize two sounds are the same, or two words are the same when spoken)
* Word Awareness (learn to recognize sentences contain smaller units called words)
* Syllable Awareness (learn to recognize words contain smaller units called syllables)
* Phonemic Awareness (learn to recognize syllables contain one or multiple distinct sounds that your voice makes)
* Rhyme & Alliteration (using all these skills in a fun and intuitive way; rhyming is about recognizing the ending phoneme of two words is the same)
* Phonemic Fun (play around with adding/changing/deleting phonemes in words)

The general idea here is that language comes natural to people _when spoken_. Children will know what language sounds like and the meaning of words, but only through _sound_. As such, to "formalize" that and turn it into rules, we need to first BREAK IT DOWN (spoken language > words, syllables, phonemes), so we can later BUILD IT BACK UP (letters > syllables, words, sentences, etcetera).

And yes. Actual letters + words were entirely moved to Level 2, even though I'd already made a nice plan for how to do them in Level 1. This was a tough decision to make. I like having language available, because it'd make the resources in level 2 much easier to make ;)

What I'm _trying_ to do, is provide an equal amount of material/topics/learning _per topic, per level_.

* The resources teaching Letters + Words are _massive_, and adding them to Level 1 too would make the Language section absolutely huge here.
* Similarly, if I kept these out of Level 2, then Language would not have much to do there. The only steps would be "learn a few more words, work with slightly longer texts"
* The importance of _sound_ in language is well-known at this point, so it feels better to _completely_ focus on that for an entire level. In that sense, the step of "orthographic mapping" (sounds -> letters; or phonemes -> graphemes) is an entirely new skill and should be moved to a new level.

I want to decouple specific skills/topics from each other, or from _age_. Instead, you simply start at Level 1 ... and once a child can do that, you move to Level 2. Their age, their speed of learning, what other things they're learning (such as maths) is irrelevant. You can never "fall behind" or have to "do exercises two grades higher than you"---in my view, this is the simplest and most freeform way to approach it all.

Nevertheless, it requires more creativity and tough decisions on my end, and I decided to keep Level 1 Language entirely focused on _sound_ and _language awareness_ (no alphabet/writing yet).


















In general, I settled on the following steps for learning language.

* (SOUND) It starts with _sound_ (spoken language). Identify sounds are the same (Discrimination needed!), identify bigger sounds (words) contain smaller sounds (syllables), rhyming is often used here.
* (LETTERS) Then you learn the actual _letter shapes_ (that belong to the sound). It's silly to present them all at once, though, or in ABC order. Similarly, there's a never-ending debate about whether to teach uppercase only or both uppercase and lowercase at once.
* (LETTERS) Now that you know individual letters, you start to _combine_ them again (= "apply special rules"). Learn about vowels/consonants, digraphs and blends.
* (WORDS) Finally, this gives you the tools to build the parts into words and sentences. This usually starts with CVC words (Consonant-Vowel-Consonant; such as CAT and DOG), then CVCe, and so forth.

One important step, however, is mentioned everywhere but usually missing from actual lesson plans/material. It's the importance of **stories**. People _know_ they should read a lot of stories to their kids, until they are ready to read a lot themselves. But how? What to do? There's usually a vague recommendation to "buy books that look fun" or "buy quality children's literature".

And so I sought to include stories as a first-class citizen from the start. It's probably why we started telling stories in the first place: to make it easier to learn and remember stuff, including language. 

It's no surprise that, in recent times, more research has surfaced that at least a good chunk of children are "gestalt learners" (instead of "analytical learners"). It means they learn language by going from BIG to SMALL: they learn individual words from stories, sentences, phrases. (Analytical means that you learn SMALL to BIG, so letters/syllables to words.) Such learners would benefit far more from hearing a lot of different stories, than from the typical path I outlined above.

And so I came up with the ludicrously ambitious plan to **write a fairy tale for every letter**.

Remember that I am using "established educational guidelines" as a way to lure people in and create traditional Teaching Resources. I don't actually agree with all of it, and I want to move on to the other factions as quickly as possible. (I am extremely explicit about this on the store itself, but of course many people don't read that and are surprised at some of the things I go for ...)

#### My First Letters (I, II, III, IV)

After quite some debate and trial-and-error, I made the following decision for teaching the letters.

**I would NOT group resources by type; instead, I'd group by LETTER and do everything at once.**

What does this mean?

* At first, I planned to do one "My First Letters" that taught all letter _shapes_. Then I'd have another "My First Stories" that included that _story_ about every letter. And so forth.
* Instead, I decided to do multiple "My First Letters". Each one only includes ~6 of the letters. But for each letter, we do _all_ the diverse exercises and approaches I had in mind.

Say I include the letter "A" with this package. Then we add ...

* The shape in uppercase and lowercase, to trace/fill/learn
* Images of things that start with or include an "A".
* The fairy tale about the letter A.
* All of this in one big poster you can hang on the wall / give a sticker if you want.

And then, at the end of the package (once you've seen all these letters), we include puzzles and quizzes about them _all_ (mixed together).

This process is almost identical for all letters/all packages, which is why I bundle all "My First Letters" in this devlog.

I sorted the letters based on consensus about the best order, but also based on practical use. After only the _first_ package, you already want children to be able to use some words, and solve some slightly more involved puzzles with real words / realistic language.

As a general guideline, you want the most commonly used vowels and consonants. (You can easily find a "letter frequency table" for any language, especially English.) But you also want to hone that subset of letters to allow as many 3-letter / 1-syllable words as possible, which are relevant to a child's daily life.

#### My First Words

This one had two tricky elements. 

* What words are simple/natural enough for young kids?
* How to play with this when someone can't be expected to read/write yet?

The first one was solved by simply checking established word lists, finding common ground, and using my own common sense. 

The second one took some creative leaps ... and the realization I was overcomplicating things.

* The "Teaching Resource" here should literally just be "IMAGE + WORD FOR IT", repeated many times :p That's how you "learn" a word in the traditional educational way. I kept forgetting that my teaching resources should provide textbook examples and references for the things they teach, instead of hastily jumping into puzzles and games already.
* At this point, kids know the letters (mostly), and they know some words, so we can jump into the established world of **word puzzles**. A simple Word Search, for example, is absolutely possible at this stage. More complicated word puzzles can be tricky, but I did my best to find out which ones were simplest and provide a nice variety of challenges that way.
  * Example: There's this simple puzzle where you have _multiple_ words with one missing letter, and you must obviously solve it by giving the missing letter. This is a sort of precursor to crosswords, which just apply this principle 30 times within the same grid.
  * Example: Instead of _writing_ the letters, I can just let you cut out the letters at the start, and solve puzzles/answer questions by picking up those tiles and placing them.

When the flood gates opened, I had _too many_ ideas and opted to move a lot of them to Level 2 > Language. Which is what I wanted to do anyway, because most Language learning beyond Level 1 is just ... "learn even more words" and "learn even longer/more complicated words".

#### My First Stories

Stories being a first-class citizen, they are already included in all the other resources. Almost every single one has 2 or 3 fairy tales in the _Explorers_ faction.

As such, this product isn't just another list of extra stories. It's about story _comprehension_, and a start to telling/producing stories _yourself_.

It took some effort to figure out the paradox of "the kids can't read/write/identify many words yet yet, but they should still understand stories and interact with them through exercises". As usual, the bridge turned out to be "real world concept > image > word".

* It starts with exercises to simply _find_ a word in a story.
* Then it turns into filling in blanks, with images giving the options. (And context has to make clear which option is the only one possible. For example, kids know that a human is not as large as a house. Thus, the sentence "He was as tall as ___" can't be completed with that.)
* Which finally turns into filling actual letters/simple words, and constructing/finishing a story yourself.

As I planned this, I felt the resource was a bit ... weak? Vague? Wishy-washy? But as I actually worked on it, I saw that my hunches were right and that this was a good thing to include. 

Yes, stories are a bit messier by default. Because they are about _context_, reading between the lines, adding _detail_ and _emotion_ to what would otherwise be an abstract list of facts. In a way, I just accepted that this resource would be more "subjective" and more "if your kid is ready for it" than others.
