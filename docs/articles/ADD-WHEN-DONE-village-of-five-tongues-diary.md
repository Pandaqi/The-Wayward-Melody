# Village of Five Tongues: An Ambitious Project

If our educational resources prove one thing, it's that you're never too young to enjoy a good puzzle, game or escape room. It _can_ be done. You _can_ create puzzles that require no text or other knowledge a young kid probably won't have. You _can_ create games that are educational, fun and absolutely simple enough for a 4-year-old to understand.

That doesn't mean it's easy, however.

Most escape rooms I've ever played and seen have a massive (over)reliance on _text_. Stories to read. Diaries to comb through. Things explained, then explained even more. Alphabet ciphers, coded words, letter substitutions, you name it. It all requires being able to read at a good pace and high level.

And I can understand that. It's much faster to write something in text than to break your brain trying to simplify it into a few clear icons. That's why we invented written language in the first place! It's easier, faster, more flexible, etcetera.

But, alas, we can't use that for our Level 1 escape rooms. All of that work has to be "pre-reader".

Then I stumbled upon an interesting game called _City of Six Moons_. It pretends to be some artefact of unknown origin, probably a game played by aliens that somehow landed here on Earth. The rules are in the alien's language. The game is literally _deciphering the game_. Bit by bit, trying to figure out what the symbols mean and how the game should work, trying to get in the minds of the aliens who designed it. It's a really creative idea, which reminded me of games like _Tunic_ too, where all tutorials are in some made-up language too. (You figure out what things mean by pressing buttons and seeing the consequences of actions.)

This got me thinking: wait a minute, _this is how you design advanced puzzles for those who can't read yet!_

To a pre-reader kid, a game rulebook written in English looks the same as one written in an alien language. They are actually _living_ that process of understanding a language for the first time.

And so, an escape room that simulates that experience (in a fun, guided, simpler way) is a _great_ educational opportunity.

That same evening I wrote down the vague idea.

> **Village of Five Tongues:** an escape room that pretends aliens left behind odd artefacts in some town. Bits and pieces of their language and how it's used. You, the players, have to figure out how their language works step by step. Basically, pre-reader kids learn the essence of "this means this, that means that, if we combine it we get more specific meaning" as they play. They see how a language is born and evolved, albeit in a heavily simplified way.

## The General Approach

Okay. Nice idea. Now ... what?

I _felt_ this would be an amazing experience. That it could work. But figuring out the specifics to such an ambitious idea was hard. Where do we even start?

I saw, as usual, two approaches.

* (OUTSIDE->INSIDE) I design the alien's language first. Then I work back in what order/how to introduce the parts.
* (INSIDE->OUTSIDE) I simply design the best/simplest puzzles I can, then try to tie the threads together into a sensible alien language near the end.

I chose the first approach, because the second one didn't make sense to me here. These players have no (strong) assumptions about language yet. Things like "let's start with communicating a number system!" makes no sense to kids who might not even be able to count yet. Similarly, things like "let's start with letters!" means nothing.

No, this was actually an opportunity to break free of that mold. To showcase an alien language very _different_ from our own. In fact, the more I worked on it, the more I imagined this as an escape room adults could enjoy too. Because they would probably be blindsided by their assumptions (based on their own language) and go down the wrong track.

{{% remark %}}
This also means the escape room is LANGUAGE INDEPENDENT. It doesn't matter what your mother tongue is. The bits of explanation/guidance around it are in English, of course, but the experience itself has no text at all. If not cool on its own, it's at least nice for marketing, right?
{{% /remark %}}

I must note this was partially inspired by seeing the movie _Arrival_. Although I'm not sure what to think about the movie and how little time it eventually devoted to the language aspect, it _did_ throw up some interesting ideas how alien species might construe language in a VERY different way.

## Designing An Alien Language

Letters/Alphabet were off the table. The players don't care/know, it would be too similar to our own languages, and you're not teaching that many individual symbols in a short escape room.

Different verb tenses, adverbs, and similar were also too complicated. You're not teaching someone what "happily" is before they even know the word for any emotion in your language.

In basically all research I've seen, the act of learning a language happens in the same way.

* Someone shows the thing.
* Then they make the sound/write the word for the thing.
* Your brain makes the connection
* And with enough connections, you develop understanding of the _deeper_ rules ("why" is it spelled/written like that)

In other words,

* I draw an image of someone walking. I write the symbol for "walk" below it.
* I draw an image of one large thing and one small thing. I write the symbols for "large" and "small" below it.
* Etcetera.

I felt this was probably a necessity here. It's just the fastest, most natural way to do it in a printed escape room. (If I had sound/film/something else, or it was for an older age bracket, I could try something completely different. But not now.)

_But_ that doesn't mean we have to stick to this "dictionary" style for all words. The escape room shouldn't just be a long list of symbols to teach to players, naming more and more specific stuff.

No, instead, I decided the alien language worked from **vague to specific**. 

* This was inspired by reading my own notes saying OUTSIDE->INSIDE ;)
* It was also inspired by reading how English and Dutch are very noun-based languages, but other languages actually aren't. They don't have a word for "airplane", but just call it "iron in the sky" or something.

In other words, the aliens barely have any specific words for specific things. Instead, they start with a broad category, then define it further, then define it further, and so forth. This is all just _one_ blended symbol.

For example, say they want to communicate **SHEEP**.

* First symbol = ANIMAL/LIVING BEING
* Second symbol = FOUR LEGS
* Third symbol = WHITE
* Fourth symbol = SOFT

These symbols combine into one "glyph". (Though, to keep things simple, they will do so in "obvious" ways with more than enough contrast to pick out the underlying symbols.)

Another example, say they want to communicate **RUN**.

* First symbol = ACTION
* Second symbol = WALK
* Third symbol = FAST

If it were a spoken language, they'd basically say "I do a _walk fast action_".

This felt like a really solid, interesting, and very workable approach.

Now ... how do we turn it into puzzles? Which gradually escalate in difficulty?

## Turning It Into Puzzles

Now I put my writer's hat on. The premise "oh we found a foreign language, let's figure it out" is ... okay, but weak. No conflict. No stakes. No clearly defined goal. (When are you done "figuring it out"? _Why_ are we doing this?)

Instead, let's start with more of a story.

* The aliens look angry, threatening, intimidating.
* They left this mysterious message (burning the crops to draw it or whatever).
* They're clearly not leaving.
* So ... _figure out what they want, then give it to them_.

The entire escape room is about figuring out that they, I dunno, lost something and want it back. So you can end the escape room with an actual action: giving that piece back to "the aliens". You can end it with this big realization what their angry message meant and solving it, the aliens leaving in peace. (Would probably be cool if I actually placed an alien spaceship on the table that you could make "fly away" ...)

As you explore the area, you stumble upon a **crashed spaceship**. Bits and pieces lying around. Fragments of their language, their stuff, scattered around the area. **All the puzzles are objects like that.**

Every puzzle is just another artefact you find. 

* Maybe you find a broken vase. You realize another part matches. Together, they seem to tell a story, like those mural paintings in Ancient Greece/Rome/Egypt.
* Maybe you find something with food stains on it. You realize it's a ledger counting food, perhaps describing what snacks they needed for a party ;) It's how you learn about the general symbol for food and how they describe numbers.
* And so forth

I want to keep it kid-friendly, of course. Light-hearted. That's why I mention the "party snacks"---that's a much better way for kids to understand than to have it be about "oh no, remember the time we STARVED TO DEATH!?"

The fragments should be children's toys, children's games, common objects/events that kids would recognize.

It should be physical. Something to pass around, study at all angles. In that sense, the **food** thing might be even better communicated by them _actually finding food_.

* The players would realize the food looks the same as those icons.
* They might notice the _amount_ of food is the same as what that fragment indicates.
* (It would normalize having quite a large number of individual objects, so it isn't too easy to "guess" which one the aliens want back.)

**I fully intend to create this idea again later, but now for adults.** This version is basically the simplified, shortened version for kids. Any ideas for more complicated puzzles must move to the "advanced version", no matter how much it breaks my heart.

## Checking Answers

Okay, this leaves one final piece unresolved. The important last step of every escape room: **a way to check your answers**.

Especially kids need these intermediate rewards. They need to regularly cheer for solving a puzzle and a confirmation they're on the right track.

How do we do this? How do we do this in an escape room that is about _slowly figuring out what symbols mean_? We can't do the usual things, such as having each puzzle end with a number/letter/word. 

My first thought was a **decoder** (or "codex"). I'd design several rotating circles such that, if you input the correct individual symbols, some general icon pops out. If this icon matches the puzzle you're solving now---congratulations! If not, keep searching.

Escape rooms use decoders, usually, so that _everyone can play_. Nobody has to know the answers or check them, which means they can't enjoy the escape room themselves.

Was that necessary here, though? This is an activity for in class, for the family, for a birthday party. It would be _expected_ that some adult was around who could "lead" this activity.

I would rather keep a complex decoder for the "advanced version" of this idea. For this version, we do the much simpler decoder: ask the Guardian ;)

_Every_ puzzle asks you to give something back to the aliens. (Or at least "pick" one or more objects. This makes it all very physical and tangible.) The players simply give their object to the Guardian; they check the answer sheet if they're right.

In my experience, silly simple solutions like that are usually _the best solutions_. It keeps things incredibly simple. It keeps the Guardian in an active role, allowing them to give hints and such too. It opens up more types of puzzles because there is this human fail-safe behind it.

And so I went for that!

Now the plan was complete.

* I knew the core of the alien's language.
* I knew the structure of each puzzle and what it should lead to.
* I knew how answers were checked and how you progressed from puzzle to puzzle.

Now it was time ... to invent the actual puzzles and their specifics.
