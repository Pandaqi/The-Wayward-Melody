# Language Notes

## Phonological Awareness

In general, kids actually learn language through _spoken sentences in real life_. As such, to "formalize" this and advance this understanding, we usually take two steps: first BREAK IT DOWN (into words, syllables, phonemes, letters), then BUILD IT BACK UP (into letters, blends/digraphs/vowels/consonants, words, sentences, ...)

At this point, kids need NO knowledge at all about the alphabet or letter shapes.

@SOURCE: Nice guidelines for when this awareness (and moving into alphabet) should be, by age => https://www.phonics.org/what-phonics-rules-should-children-know-by-age/

After this we can move from Phoneme => Grapheme (letter representation).

"Counting" here just means clapping, stomping, raising fingers/hands, jumping, grabbing/moving physical objects, etcetera.

These follow the "I do, We do, You do" protocol mostly => Make this a _general_ teaching tip (some extra "How to Teach" resource?), or include specifically with each exercise?

Often, the different parts are represented by _different objects_ first. (Such as actual different tiles in size/color, one for ONSET and one for RIME.) You touch the object representing a "part" of a word and make the sound. Once clear, you push the objects together and say the whole word/blended sound.

## Research

**Language has four processing systems:**

* Phonological => sounds we can make
* Orthographic => maps sounds to symbols/alphabet
* Meaning => turns a combination of letters/sounds into meaning. 
* Context => looks at other language _around_ it (which modifies or finalizes word meaning).

**Unlike speaking, reading is not a natural process and must be taught.** 

* English is an alphabetic writing system where the letters represent the speech sounds. 
* Most children need instruction in the _connection_ between the letters and the phonemes, and for many, this link needs to be explicitly taught.

**For every later rule we introduce (such as FLOSS, the R-changing thing, Schwa, etcetera), include 1 or 2 "decodable books".** 
* These only use rule(s) taught so far, to allow you to figure out a word's sound/spoken form on your own. Which means reading independently as a kid from a young age.
  * In practice, this means a low number of "Phoneme-Grapheme" relations. (There are 44 phonemes in English.)
  * It means word types are mostly simple and the same. (Only CVC and CVCe for example, with just a single CCVCVC)
  * Any potentially hard thing (such as a digraph) should be MARKED to indicate to a child "this is special/this is not what you think it is"
* Do NOT use repetitive books; these just cause superficial memorization as they're predictable.
